India is adding renewable capacity at remarkable speed. Solar tariffs have
fallen to levels that would have looked implausible a decade ago, and new
plants are being commissioned faster than the grid was designed to absorb.

The interesting problem is no longer generation. It is timing.

## The shape of the problem

A solar plant produces hardest around noon. Demand peaks in the evening, after
the sun has gone. Those two curves have never matched, and every additional
megawatt of solar makes the mismatch larger rather than smaller.

Up to a point, the grid absorbs this. Past that point, three things start
happening:

- **Curtailment.** The grid cannot take the output, so a plant that is
  perfectly capable of generating is told not to. The asset earns nothing for
  those hours.
- **Falling capture prices.** When every solar plant in a region generates at
  the same time, the value of energy in that window falls. Plants compete
  against each other into the same oversupplied hour.
- **Fossil backup.** The evening peak still has to be served. Without storage,
  it is served by something that burns fuel — which undoes a meaningful share
  of what the renewable build-out was meant to achieve.

## What storage actually changes

A battery sitting at the point of connection decouples *when power is
generated* from *when it is delivered*. That single change addresses all three
problems at once. Surplus midday generation is captured instead of curtailed.
It is sold into the evening, when it is worth more. And the peak is served
without a diesel set or a gas peaker.

It also does something less obvious but increasingly valuable: it lets a plant
commit to a **schedule** rather than a forecast. As grid codes tighten, the
ability to deliver a promised profile — regardless of cloud cover — moves from
a nice-to-have to a condition of connection.

## Why this is an engineering problem, not a procurement one

It is tempting to treat storage as a box to be bought. In practice the useful
question is not "how many MWh?" but "what duty cycle does this asset need to
serve, for how many years, under what ambient conditions?"

Those answers determine chemistry, thermal design, cycle depth and warranty
structure. Get them wrong and the system either costs more than it needed to,
or quietly loses capacity years before anyone budgeted for it.

That is the work: sizing a system against the case it actually has to serve,
and then standing behind it through commissioning and the two decades that
follow.
